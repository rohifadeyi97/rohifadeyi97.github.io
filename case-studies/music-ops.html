01 / EVOLUTION

From automated playlists to a shared workspace

The autonomous playlisting workflow came first: recurring music discovery, selection, maintenance, publishing, and reconciliation across branded playlists. It gained a second life when I reused the same catalog and recommendation logic for the Vinyl Club workflow.

Over time, that backend grew bigger than a scheduled script should feel. Reviewing email, approval decisions, playlist states, content ideas, and the files being created meant moving between too many places. The newer Music Ops dashboard is the visible front end of that same system. It surfaces the work instead of hiding it.

The expansion matters because the editorial output grew beyond playlists: recommendations can inform newsletter and YouTube concepts, with thumbnails, carousels, and motion outputs feeding into a categorized content library. File locations, source evidence, and human review are part of the interface—not cleanup work left for later.

02 / THE ORIGINAL SYSTEM

The original playlist and Vinyl Club processes

The original problem was playlist maintenance. Five Who's AUX playlists and four Intelligent Design playlists each had their own theme, refresh cadence, and definition of what belonged. Keeping them current required discovery, metadata cleanup, duplicate checks, scoring, rotation, publishing, and manual review.

Once that infrastructure was working, I reused it for Vinyl Club. Instead of building a separate recommendation tool, I connected the monthly editorial workflow to the same catalog, provider mappings, history, and research data. That made it possible to go from an album link in an email to a researched shortlist, an approved playlist, Spotify and Apple publishing, and a content brief without rebuilding the process every month.

03 / DATA FOUNDATION

Music catalog and data sources

Discovery comes from Apple Music charts, Apple seed graphs, editorial seed playlists, Last.fm, ListenBrainz, and existing catalog observations. Spotify is used more selectively for corroboration, identity checks, and playability. I also built two local MusicBrainz SQLite reference databases, roughly 42 GB combined, so canonical artist, release, recording, work, credit, and relationship data can be queried locally instead of repeatedly calling a remote API.

The working SQLite database separates source observations from normalized tracks and workflow state. It stores provenance, theme signals, playlist membership, manual edit events, observed provider state, recommendation runs, email events, album identifiers, approved monthly tracks, and publishing history. That separation lets the system reuse previous work while still showing where a decision came from.

Schema in practice

Source layer Raw artist/title observations, provider keys, charts, seed relationships, listening signals
↓
Normalized catalog Tracks, identities, ISRCs, source provenance, cached resolutions, MusicBrainz relationships
↓
Operational state Theme scores, membership, suppression, manual events, recommendation runs, email queue, month tracks, publish history
  • `track_sources` and `track_signals` preserve source evidence
  • `manual_playlist_events` records human additions and removals
  • `playlist_observed_state` tracks what is actually on-platform
  • `vinyl_email_events` provides a durable webhook queue
  • `vinyl_month_tracks` stores the approved monthly playlist state
04 / SCHEDULED OPERATIONS

Scheduled playlist maintenance

01 / Ingest

Find and store new candidates

Weekly ingestion pulls Apple chart data, walks theme-specific seed graphs, adds Last.fm and discovery signals, and writes source observations into the shared catalog.

02 / Resolve

Clean up identity once

Candidate tracks are normalized and corroborated across sources. Cached provider and MusicBrainz identities prevent the same artist or recording from being resolved from scratch on every run.

03 / Refresh

Apply playlist-specific rules

Each playlist uses its own qualification logic. Throwbacks enforces a ten-year age rule, Anthems emphasizes recognition, and the other themes use seed and source alignment with artist, recurrence, and cooldown controls.

04 / Sync + reconcile

Keep automation from fighting the editor

Apple Music sync updates eligible playlists, while reconciliation observes manual additions and removals and stores them as durable events. Quality reporting shows what is ready, waiting, or blocked.

05 / VINYL CLUB

Vinyl Club recommendation and publishing workflow

01 / EMAIL

Start with a link Resend receives the message and posts it to a webhook. The event is written to a durable SQLite queue before processing.

02 / RESEARCH

Resolve and recommend The anchor album is normalized, then local MusicBrainz relationships plus Apple, Last.fm, ListenBrainz, and bounded Spotify checks build a shortlist with stored evidence.

03 / PICK

Keep the decision human The editor selects two records. Spotify album identities and track sets are frozen so the approved decision does not shift later.

04 / DRAFT

Build and review The playlist combines representative tracks from the three records with discovery-first expansion. Exact IDs, ISRCs, fingerprints, artist caps, and album caps protect quality.

05 / PUBLISH

Ship and report Spotify publishes the approved draft, Apple publishes the confidently resolved subset, and a Monthly Content Packet is generated from the final playlist and frozen recommendation evidence.
06 / WHAT CHANGED

Operational outcomes

01 / Scale

9 playlists / 2 brands

One shared data model supports different themes, qualification rules, and refresh schedules.

02 / Efficiency

Less repeated research

Source observations, identities, relationships, and prior decisions are stored once and reused across future runs.

03 / Control

Human-in-the-loop

Automation handles the repeatable work, while editors still control final taste, overrides, and approval.

04 / Reuse

One system, more output

The same foundation now supports playlist maintenance, research, monthly publishing, workflow history, and content preparation.
