01 / Overview

Why the integrations were needed

The organization already had functioning systems for CRM, ecommerce, course registrations, website inquiries, and marketing. Each worked for its immediate task, but the information needed to run the business was spread across separate workflows, field definitions, and identifiers.

That made ordinary questions expensive to answer. A lead could arrive with useful campaign context but no consistent follow-up path. A product could exist in both CRM and ecommerce with pricing represented differently. Registration records connected people, companies, courses, and purchases, but those relationships were difficult to analyze together. The work focused on turning those transactions into one dependable operational spine.

02 / Approach

Integration architecture

Salesforce became the central relationship and operational layer. Website forms captured intent and attribution, then transformed visitor-friendly values into CRM-compatible formats. Salesforce automation handled ownership, notifications, and follow-up. Product workflows queried core product and price-book data, Zapier normalized child records into explicit fields, and a custom WordPress REST endpoint validated create-versus-update behavior before writing to WooCommerce.

Registration workflows followed the same principle without forcing every business object through one monolithic process: leads, products, and registrations used separate automation paths, then converged through governed identifiers and shared relationships. Names stayed descriptive; stable product, registration, contact, account, course, and order keys did the matching.

03 / Operational output

Implemented workflows

01

Lead lifecycle

One reusable intake framework captures identity, intent, topic interest, open context, and campaign attribution, then creates a Salesforce Lead, assigns ownership, alerts the right people, and creates a follow-up activity.

02

Commerce & pricing

Product data moves from Salesforce into WooCommerce with standard and member prices separated explicitly, product families aligned to categories, fulfillment attributes preserved, and endpoint-level create/update controls.

03

Registration relationships

Participants, accounts, courses, registrations, products, and orders can be connected through stable identifiers, supporting variable attendee counts without losing the relationships needed for participation and revenue analysis.

04

Attribution & reporting

Campaign parameters and normalized operational fields create a bridge from marketing activity to CRM outcomes, while cleaner product and registration data supports future pipeline, demand, pricing, fulfillment, and customer analysis.

04 / REFLECTION

Lessons and next steps

The hardest failures were rarely the API calls themselves. They came from assumptions about the data: multi-select values arriving in the wrong delimiter, child price-book records behaving like one field, line-item arrays needing explicit loops, environment-specific IDs breaking matches, and incomplete attributes quietly moving downstream.

I would now front-load the data contract before building the automation: define a field dictionary, assign a source of truth to each field, choose stable cross-system identifiers, document create-versus-update rules, and decide which exceptions must be visible in reporting. I would also keep distinct business-object workflows separate. Leads, products, and registrations should not be one giant automation.

Design principles carried forward

01Model before mappingDefine fields, types, ownership, and valid values first.
02Names are not keysUse stable external identifiers for matching and migration.
03Transform before transmitNormalize arrays, multi-selects, prices, and metadata before posting.
04Exceptions are dataMissing prices, links, categories, or dimensions should be measurable.
